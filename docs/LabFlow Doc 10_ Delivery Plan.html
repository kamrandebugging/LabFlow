1.5 Assumptions

AS-1. The builder and the operator are the same solo developer Docs 01–09 assume. Every procedure here (launch, weekly review, monthly drill) is sized for one person and stated in hours, not team-days.

AS-2. Phase durations in §4 are planning estimates for one developer working full-time. They are estimates, not commitments: the exit gates are the commitment, the week ranges are the forecast.

AS-3. Pilot scale means the first handful of tenants — up to about 5 labs — with platform email volume in the low hundreds per day. Every free-tier decision in this document is made for pilot scale and carries a measured trigger for the day it stops fitting (§7, §8.2).

AS-4. Prices in §2 were checked on 2026-10-20 against the cited pages. Where sources disagreed (VPS pricing after the June 2026 adjustments), §6 budgets a class ceiling instead of a point price.

2. Research findings

Findings are numbered F-1 to F-9 and cited where the document uses them. Anything that could not be sourced is marked Unverified and is not used as the basis for a decision.

F-1 — VPS prices moved in 2026; budget the class, not the price

A Hetzner CX22 (2 vCPU, 4 GB RAM, 40 GB disk, 20 TB traffic) — the class of machine Doc 01's G3 is measured on — is listed at about €3.99/month in a 2026 review of Hetzner Cloud pricing. Hetzner then adjusted prices on 15 June 2026: the CPX22 (same 2 vCPU / 4 GB shape, newer hardware) rose from €7.99 to €19.49/month. The same 2 vCPU / 4 GB shape costs $24/month at DigitalOcean, Linode and Lightsail, and $20/month at Vultr. Two lessons: the cost-optimised class is still cheap, and any exact price printed in a design document will be wrong within a year — so §6 budgets a ceiling for the class and §5.2 records the purchase-day price as the fact that matters. Sources: Hetzner Review 2026 — pricing breakdown; VPS price tracking through the 2026 increases.

F-2 — Brevo's free tier is a daily cap: 300 emails/day, transactional included

Brevo's free plan sends 300 emails/day with unlimited contacts, and the cap is shared between marketing and transactional mail on the same account; SMTP access is included, and no credit card is required. The first paid tier (Starter) starts at $9/month for 5,000 emails/month and removes the daily cap; higher tiers scale by monthly sends. For a platform whose mail is all transactional (report links, notices, statements) and sent through one account (Doc 06 AS-4), the daily cap — not the monthly total — is the binding number, and §8.2's triggers are written against it. Sources: Brevo pricing 2026: free, Starter and Business plans; Brevo as an SMTP server — pricing.

F-3 — Amazon SES is the cheap scale path, with an account-setup cost

Amazon SES à-la-carte sending costs $0.10 per 1,000 emails with no monthly minimum; since 21 July 2026 new accounts start on an Essentials plan at $0.16 per 1,000 and can switch back to à la carte. At LabFlow's plausible grown-up volume — say 100,000 emails/month — SES costs about $10/month in sending, far below any hosted tier. The cost is operational instead: an AWS account, sandbox-exit approval, and bounce/complaint handling the platform must watch itself. That is why SES is §8.3's documented scale path and not the launch provider: at pilot volume the saving is a few dollars and the setup is days. Sources: Amazon SES pricing and free tier 2026; What changed in SES pricing plans.

F-4 — Sentry's free plan is 5,000 error events a month, one user

Sentry's Developer plan is free, allows one user, and includes 5,000 error events and 10,000 performance spans per month with 30-day retention. The Team plan starts at $26/month with 50,000 error events included. Doc 08 §11.1 sends 100% of error events unsampled, so the free quota is a hard ceiling that error volume — not tenant count directly — consumes; §7.3 turns that into a measured trigger. Sources: Sentry pricing; Sentry plans and free-tier contents.

F-5 — GitHub Free includes 2,000 Actions minutes a month for private repos

GitHub Free includes 2,000 Actions minutes per month for private repositories (GitHub Pro raises this to 3,000); beyond the allowance, standard Linux runners bill $0.008/minute, and jobs on self-hosted runners carry no per-minute charge. With a $0 spending limit, jobs stop when the allowance is exhausted instead of billing. Doc 09's pipeline target is under 15 minutes per PR run; §17 does the arithmetic against this allowance. Sources: GitHub's plans — GitHub Docs; GitHub Actions guide — pricing section.

F-6 — Cloudflare R2's free tier covers v1's storage shape

R2's free tier includes 10 GB-month of storage, 1 million Class A (write) operations and 10 million Class B (read) operations per month, with egress free on every tier; beyond the tier, storage is $0.015 per GB-month. Doc 01 §15 estimates roughly 150 KB per report, so the free storage tier alone holds tens of thousands of report PDFs before backups and exports are counted — storage cost is not a launch constraint, and §6 records it as $0 at pilot scale with the per-GB rate as the growth rule. Source: R2 pricing — Cloudflare Docs.

F-7 — A .app domain costs about $14/year at renewal; first-year prices mislead

Tracked .app pricing across registrars shows renewal — the price paid every year — at about $14.20/year at at-cost registrars such as Cloudflare Registrar, while headline first-year offers run lower and some registrars renew above $20/year. Doc 01 §17 already names domain expiry as a risk with "multi-year domain" as the mitigation; §5.1 therefore fixes the registrar class (at-cost, flat renewal), a 5-year initial term, and auto-renew — the decision is about the renewal price and the term, not the first-year discount. Sources: Cheapest .app domain registrar 2026; .app at Cloudflare — registration and renewal prices.

F-8 — The VPS region is a measurement, not a preference

Hetzner Cloud operates locations in Germany, Finland, Singapore and the USA. LabFlow's users are in Pakistan; the closest of those locations on the map is not automatically the fastest route, and no source consulted gives a trustworthy Pakistan-to-datacenter latency figure — any number printed here would be Unverified. §5.2 therefore fixes the procedure (measure from Pakistan on provisioning day, pick the lowest, keep the choice reversible inside the first week because billing is hourly) instead of naming a region. Source for the location list: Hetzner Cloud review 2026 — datacenters.

F-9 — One Beat process, crontab schedules, fan-out by task

Celery Beat is a single scheduler process that sends due tasks to the queues; periodic work is declared with crontab schedules, and only one Beat may run. This is Doc 05 F-7, restated because §9's timetable depends on it: every periodic job in LabFlow is one Beat entry fanning out to per-tenant child tasks (Doc 04 §13.2), and the only freedom left — the one OQ-45 reserves — is the minute each entry fires at. Source: Celery periodic tasks documentation, as cited in Doc 05 F-7.

3. Delivery principles

DP-1 — Scope is frozen. v1 is exactly what Docs 01–09 specify — no more. Doc 01 §17 names scope creep as this project's highest-likelihood risk and assigns the fix to this document; the fix is this rule. A new idea found during the build goes to the post-v1 backlog (§22) with a note, never into the current phase. The only exception is a defect: work needed to make a specified behaviour true is in scope by definition.

DP-2 — A phase ends at its gate, not at a date. Each phase in §4 exits when its scenario suites pass in CI (Doc 09 §9.2's traceability rule), not when its estimated weeks run out. A phase that is "90% built" with a red suite is not done, and the next phase does not start on top of it — in a schema-per-tenant system, building phase N+1 over a broken phase N multiplies the repair by every tenant created meanwhile.

DP-3 — Build in dependency order; the frontend and the security layer are not phases. The build order follows the documents' own dependency order (§4). Doc 07's screens are built inside the phase that owns their backend behaviour, screen by screen, so every phase ends with something usable. Doc 08's controls are likewise built with the module they protect (throttles with login, the scrubber with the first Sentry wiring); Phase 5 then proves them end-to-end as a set. A separate "frontend phase" or "security phase" at the end is how both end up shallow.

DP-4 — Free tiers first, each with a written exit trigger. Doc 01's constraint is a zero-budget build on free tiers and open source. This document keeps that rule and makes it safe: every free tier adopted in §5–§8 and §17 is paired with the measured signal that says it has stopped fitting, and the paid step that follows. A free tier without an exit trigger is a surprise outage scheduled for later.

DP-5 — One VPS until a trigger fires. The platform runs on one VPS (Doc 01 §15) until a §7 trigger — not a feeling, not a tenant's request — says otherwise. Each trigger names the next step in Doc 01 §16's scaling order. Premature splitting is the symmetric failure to late splitting: a solo operator pays for every extra host every week, in patching and in drills, whether it is needed or not.

DP-6 — Rehearse the bad days before the first tenant. Backups are proven by a restore, the deploy by a rollback, and the launch by the checklist in §12 — all before a real lab depends on any of them. The pilot (§13) starts only when §12's gates pass. A solo operator has no team to improvise with during an incident; the rehearsal is the team.

DP-7 — Operate on a cadence, not on anxiety. §14 fixes what is checked daily, weekly and monthly, and how long each check should take. Everything Docs 04, 06 and 08 need watched is on that list, with its alert or its review. If a signal is not on the cadence and has no alert, it is not being watched — and §14 says so rather than pretending otherwise.

4.2 Why this order

Tenancy before features. Phase 0 builds the skeleton because every later module assumes schema context, provisioning and the ask endpoint exist. Retrofitting tenancy under finished features is the one reorder this plan forbids: Doc 04's rules (TP-4, per-schema migrations) are cheapest when there is nothing to retrofit.

Money after the clinical path. Revenue share (Phase 3) consumes payments, and payments exist only once billing (Phase 1) is true. Building the ledger first would mean building it against imagined payment behaviour.

Automation after the events exist. n8n workflows (Phase 4) subscribe to events Docs 03 and 05 emit. The workflows are quick to build once the events are real, and untestable before.

Hardening as a gate, not a start. Doc 08's individual controls are built with their modules (DP-3), but the SE suite is run as one gate in Phase 5 — because several controls (the scrubber, the throttle set, the audit contract) only prove themselves against the whole surface at once.

5. Infrastructure decisions: domain, VPS, staging

5.1 The domain (OQ-17, part 1)

Decision. Buy a real domain in the .app class Doc 01 assumes, from an at-cost registrar with flat renewal pricing (Cloudflare Registrar is the reference example, F-7), for an initial term of 5 years, with auto-renew on and expiry alerts at 90, 30 and 7 days. The wildcard DNS and on-demand TLS design (Doc 01 §15, Doc 04 §6.4) needs no other DNS product: one wildcard A record, no wildcard certificate, no DNS token.

What this document does not do is print a chosen name. labflow.app is Doc 01's placeholder; whether it — or a better available name — is the one bought is decided at the registrar on purchase day, by availability and price. What is fixed here is everything that makes the choice safe: the TLD class (HTTPS-enforced, matching the platform's HSTS posture, Doc 08 §8), the renewal price class (~$14.20/year, F-7), the 5-year term (Doc 01 §17's "multi-year domain" mitigation), and the alerts (an expired domain takes every tenant host down at once; it is the cheapest catastrophic failure to prevent).

Failure mode. If the preferred name is unavailable or premium-priced, the rule is: choose another clean name in the same class rather than a cheap first-year offer at a high-renewal registrar — the 5-year cost table (F-7) is the comparison, never the first-year price.

5.2 The VPS (OQ-17, part 2)

Decision. Launch on one Hetzner Cloud cost-optimised VPS of the class Doc 01's G3 is stated on: 2 vCPU / 4 GB RAM / 40 GB disk (CX22 class, F-1), hourly billed. The region is chosen on provisioning day by measurement (F-8): from a connection in Pakistan, measure latency to the candidate locations (Falkenstein, Nuremberg, Helsinki, Singapore), pick the lowest, and record the measurement and the result in the capacity log (§7.1). Because billing is hourly and nothing tenant-facing exists yet, the choice is reversible inside the first week at a cost of cents: if the measured region performs badly under the Phase 6 k6 run, move before the pilot, not after.

Fallback. The Compose stack is provider-agnostic (Doc 01 §15), so the provider is replaceable without any design change. If a Hetzner account cannot be opened or the class is unavailable, the pre-approved fallback is the same 2 vCPU / 4 GB shape at DigitalOcean or Vultr ($24 and $20/month respectively, F-1) — more expensive, identical architecture. No third option is evaluated at launch; provider-shopping is not a phase.

Sizing honesty. 2 vCPU / 4 GB runs the whole Compose stack (Doc 01 §15) at pilot scale (AS-3). It is not sized for growth, and §7's triggers — not optimism — decide when it changes. The first upgrade step is a bigger VPS in the same class line (4 vCPU / 8 GB), which is a resize, not a migration; splitting services comes later in Doc 01 §16's order.

5.3 Staging (OQ-55)

Decision. No staging host at launch. Pre-release verification for v1 is: local Compose with dev/prod parity (Doc 09 §4.4), the full pipeline plus the nightly full Playwright suite on main (Doc 09 §12), and the synthetic demo tenants (Doc 09 DG-17) on the production host for tenant-shaped verification with synthetic data only. This is Doc 09 §4.3's default, now confirmed as the launch plan rather than left as an assumption.

Why this is safe enough for the pilot. The dangerous operation in this system is not a code deploy — CI rehearses those — it is a per-schema migration. That risk is carried by Doc 09's migration gates (drift check, expand/contract, DG-22's halt-on-failure) and Doc 04's TN-15/TN-17, all of which run without a staging host. A second host would rehearse the same migrations against the same synthetic data CI already uses; at pilot scale it buys little and costs a second box to patch, back up and drill.

The trigger that adds staging. A staging host (same VPS class as production, §6) is added before the first release that (a) ships to a paying tenant, and (b) carries a contract-step migration (Doc 09 §7.3) — whichever release first meets both conditions. From that release on, migrations rehearse on staging against a restored copy of the largest tenant's schema (Doc 04 §14's restore procedure) before they run in production. Until the trigger fires, §5.3's second paragraph is the whole staging story, and no procedure in this document may quietly assume a staging host exists.

7.3 The Sentry quota trigger (OQ-52)

Decision. There is no honest tenant count at which the free quota breaks, and this document does not invent one: Doc 08 §11.1 sends error events unsampled, so the quota is consumed by error volume, and one looping bug in one tenant burns more than ten quiet tenants combined (F-4). The trigger is therefore the quota itself, read weekly (§14.2):

  • Watch: month-to-date events project past 3,500 (70% of 5,000) at the current rate.
  • Act: projection past 4,000 (80%), or two consecutive months ending above 3,500. First, cut noise at the source — Doc 08 §11.4's quiet classes and any single issue dominating the count get fixed or filtered first, because a paid plan bought to store one bug's loop is waste. If the volume is real signal after that pass, move to Sentry Team ($26/month, 50,000 events, F-4) and add the line to §6.
  • Self-hosting Sentry is the alternative Doc 08 §18 already defers: it is reconsidered only if Team pricing itself becomes the problem, because a self-hosted Sentry is a second platform to patch on the same one-person cadence (§14).

During the CSP report-only period (§12.3), csp_report events count against the same quota. The weekly review reads them as their own line: a browser-extension noise spike is expected and is not, by itself, an Act trigger.

7.4 Object storage growth

R2 growth is the slowest-moving number in the log: at roughly 150 KB per report (Doc 01 §15) plus nightly per-schema dumps inside Doc 08 §12's retention (7 daily + 4 weekly), the free 10 GB (F-6) covers pilot scale for a long time. No trigger is needed beyond the weekly GB reading; past the free tier the cost is $0.015 per GB-month, which stays a rounding error until stored data reaches the hundreds of GB — by which point §7.2's disk trigger will already have fired for the database, and storage is reviewed in the same conversation.

8. Email operations: provider, limits, message_id

8.1 The provider (OQ-46)

Decision. Brevo sends all LabFlow email in v1, through the single platform SMTP account Doc 06 AS-4 assumes, held as credentials in n8n (Doc 06's design: Django never talks to the provider; n8n does). The sending domain is authenticated (SPF, DKIM, DMARC records on the platform domain, §5.1) before the pilot's first real email. Doc 06's protocol is provider-agnostic by design, so this choice touches no payload, event or callback — it is a credential and a DNS record, which is exactly why Doc 06 could defer it safely.

Why Brevo and not the alternatives. The free tier needs no credit card and includes transactional SMTP today (F-2) — the launch constraint (Doc 01: zero budget) is met without a sales process or an AWS account. Amazon SES is cheaper per email at volume but costs days of setup and approval at pilot scale to save single-digit dollars (F-3); it is kept as the documented scale path (§8.3). SendGrid's free plan no longer exists for new accounts (a 60-day trial at 100/day, per F-3's comparison sources), so it fails the launch constraint outright.

8.2 The free-tier limits and what happens at them (OQ-47)

The limit that binds is 300 emails/day across the whole platform account (F-2). LabFlow's daily volume is: report-ready emails and link resends (Doc 03), sample-collected and rejection notices (Doc 06), password resets (Doc 08 A-27's event, delivered the same way), settlement statements (monthly, after approval — §10), and platform mail to owners. At pilot scale (AS-3: up to ~5 labs) a busy platform day sits in the low hundreds — inside the cap, without headroom to waste.

What hitting the cap looks like. A provider-side throttle is not a new failure shape: sends fail softly at n8n, delivery rows age, and the existing 15-minute sweep retries them (Doc 06 §9) — the mail goes out late, not never, and the ageing rows are visible in the weekly review (§14.2). 8.4 message_id, finally (OQ-48)

Decision. Doc 06's interim rule is confirmed as the final v1 rule, unchanged. Against the chosen provider the facts are: Brevo's SMTP/API response carries a message identifier for an accepted message; where the provider's response to n8n exposes it, n8n includes message_id in the callback; where it does not, the field is omitted. It is optional, never required, never invented, logged with the callback, and not persisted on the delivery row in v1 — Doc 02 has no column for it and none is proposed (§21). If a future support dispute ever needs per-message tracing against the provider's logs, the callback logs plus the provider's own dashboard cover the pilot era; a persisted column would be a Doc 02 amendment proposed then, with that dispute as its evidence.

10. Settlement fan-out on the 1st

Decision (OQ-44). No staggering in v1. The monthly run stays exactly as Doc 05 specifies: one Beat entry at 01:00 on the 1st (§9) fanning out to one task per tenant, then per partner (Doc 05 F-7). Three reasons, in order of weight:

1. Generation is not email. The run creates DRAFT settlements and renders statement PDFs. A statement is emailed only after the Owner approves it (Doc 05 §7–§8) — a staff act that happens during lab hours, spread by human behaviour across the first days of the month. The feared spike — 100 tenants' statements hitting the SMTP cap at 01:00 — cannot occur, because nothing is sent at 01:00. The email volume of the 1st is bounded by approvals (§8.2's daily reading catches it if that ever changes).

2. Fairness already exists where the load is. The run's real cost is CPU: aggregation queries and WeasyPrint renders. The renders ride the same queues as report PDFs, where Doc 04 §13.5's per-tenant concurrency cap (at most 2 concurrent renders per tenant) already stops any one tenant — including a tenant whose own generation run is large — from monopolising the workers. Staggering tenants would solve a contention the cap already solves.

3. The run has a night of slack, and a log. Drafts are needed when labs open, not at 01:05. Doc 05 §12.7's generation-run log records, per tenant, partners drafted, partners skipped, failures and run duration — the measurement this decision is reviewed against (§14.3).

11. Release and deployment procedure

The mechanics are Doc 09's (§11.5, §12.3): SemVer tag on main → CI builds images from the tag → deploy updates the Compose stack and runs tenant migrations → changelog section cut. 11.1 The deploy sequence

  1. Confirm the gates. The tag's pipeline is fully green, including every scenario suite (Doc 09 §9.2). A release that cannot show T, TN, RS, AU, FE and SE all green does not deploy.
  2. Confirm last night's backup. The backup manifest (Doc 04 §17) shows every active schema in last night's run. Never migrate on an unproven backup day.
  3. Note the migration shape. If the release carries migrations, they are expand-step or additive (Doc 09 §7.3); a contract-step is a separate, later release and — after §5.3's trigger — a staging-rehearsed one.   4. Deploy in a quiet window. Early morning Asia/Karachi, before labs open. The deploy runs migrations per schema; a failure on any schema halts the deploy (Doc 09 §17.2, TN-15) — the operator fixes forward with a new release or rolls back per step 6; no schema is hand-patched, ever.

  7. Record. Release tag, deploy duration, migration wall time and smoke result go into the capacity log (§7.1).

11.2 Rollback

Rollback is the previous tag redeployed, with migrations reversed per schema (TN-17 proves the shape). Two rules: a rollback is a release event — it is recorded, changelog-noted, and followed by a fix-forward release, never by editing the rolled-back code in place; and a release whose migrations cannot reverse (a contract step that dropped data) is not rolled back but fixed forward — which is precisely why contract steps ship alone, in quiet windows, and after §5.3's trigger, rehearsed (§5.3).

11.3 Hotfixes

Doc 09 §11.5's shape: a normal PR to main, a new PATCH tag, the same sequence as §11.1 with no steps skipped. Urgency shortens the quiet-window rule only when the bug is actively harming a tenant; the backup precondition and the smoke step are never skipped — a hotfix deployed onto an unproven backup converts one incident into the option of two.

15. Continuity and operator succession

The question (OQ-53). Doc 08 §12.2 fixes custody: the age identity never lives on the VPS and exists in two offline copies. It does not say who the second copy's holder is to be, or what happens if the operator is unavailable for an extended period. That is an operations question, answered here.

Decision — the continuity holder. The holder of the second offline age-identity copy is designated the platform's continuity holder: one named, trusted person, chosen by the operator, who is not expected to operate anything. The holder receives a sealed instruction envelope (physical, or its encrypted digital equivalent — the medium is the operator's choice; the sealing and the naming are not optional) containing:

  • What they hold: the second age identity copy, and a plain-language statement of what it unlocks (the backups, Doc 08 §12) and what it does not.
  • The recovery facts for the platform's accounts — registrar, VPS provider, GitHub, R2, Sentry, Brevo — meaning where each account lives and how the operator's own recovery works (password-manager emergency access or account recovery contacts), so a successor operator can regain control in order.
  • The instruction itself, in order: (1) do nothing while the operator is reachable; (2) if the operator is unreachable for an extended period, hand the envelope's contents to the successor the operator has named in it; (3) never use the keys, log in, or direct the platform yourself — the holder is a custodian, not a deputy operator.
  • The tenants' interest, stated on the card: labs' data sits in the backups this key unlocks; the handover exists so tenants are never locked out of their own records by one person's absence.

Why this shape. The realistic failure is not malice; it is one person in hospital for a month while a VPS bill or a domain renewal comes due. The mechanism therefore optimises for legibility to a non-technical custodian and a clean handover, not for the holder running anything. Naming the actual person inside this document would be wrong — the document is a design record, the envelope is the operational artefact — so this section fixes the role, the contents and the review (§14.4), and §12's launch gates require the envelope to exist before the pilot. Interim until then: none is needed — with no real tenant, there is nothing to succeed to; the gate is what makes "later" impossible to forget.

16. Accessibility audit plan

The question (OQ-50). Doc 07 D-2 fixes the v1 baseline (labels, visible focus, keyboard-complete flows, the §3.1 colour rules) and defers the full WCAG 2.2 AA audit as unscoped. This section owns it.

Decision. The audit runs in the first release cycle after the pilot completes — concretely, it is a named work item in the first post-pilot release (the release planned once the §13 pilot tenants are live and stable), and in any case is scheduled no later than 3 months after go-live. The reasoning is Doc 07's own: an audit against screens that are still changing weekly certifies a moving target. The pilot is when the screens stop moving weekly; that, not the calendar, is the real trigger, and the 3-month backstop stops "after the pilot" from becoming "eventually".

The matrix. The audit covers the staff app's core flows (Doc 07 §8) and the patient report page, against: NVDA + Chrome on Windows; VoiceOver + Safari on iOS; keyboard-only operation (no screen reader); 200% browser zoom with no horizontal scrolling of content; Android TalkBack as a spot check on the two highest-traffic flows. Urdu UI is audited in the same passes — RTL layout and the Doc 09 §10.2 language-stable test IDs are part of what is being certified, not an extra.

Method and output. The operator performs the audit against a checklist built from the WCAG 2.2 AA success criteria, flow by flow; every failure becomes either a fix in the same release or a FE-suite test (Doc 09 §9) plus a scheduled fix — findings are never left as a PDF nobody re-opens. Where an automated check can carry a criterion forward (contrast, labels, roles), it is added to CI in Doc 09's pipeline, so the audit's result persists as gates rather than as a one-day state. An external auditor is a budget decision, not part of this plan: if the platform's revenue ever justifies one, the matrix above is the brief they are hired against. Until then the honest claim, matching Doc 08 §18, is "baseline built in, full audit performed against this matrix on this schedule" — no compliance certification is claimed anywhere.

17. CI hosting economics

The question (OQ-54). Doc 09's pipeline — full suite on every merge, nightly full Playwright — runs on GitHub Actions. Hosted minutes are finite on the Free plan (F-5); a self-hosted runner is unmetered but, per Doc 09 §12.4 and Doc 08's trust boundary, must never be the production VPS.

The arithmetic. Doc 09 §12.4 targets under 15 minutes for the PR pipeline. At a solo developer's merge cadence — planning figure: up to 40 PR/merge runs a month — that is ≈ 600 minutes. The nightly full suite (full Playwright, FE-01–FE-20 plus the smoke already counted in PR runs) is the larger consumer: planning figure 30 runs × ~30 minutes ≈ 900 minutes. Total planning figure ≈ 1,500 minutes/month against the Free plan's 2,000 (F-5). These are planning figures, not measurements; the weekly review (§14.2) replaces them with the real numbers from the first month onward.

Decision. Stay on GitHub-hosted runners on the Free plan, with the account's spending limit set to $0 — so exhaustion stops jobs rather than billing (F-5), turning an overage from a surprise invoice into a visible, fixable event. Triggers:

  • Watch: measured usage passes 1,600 minutes (80%) in a month — first response is waste removal (cancel-in-progress on PR runs, caching), not payment.
  • Act: usage passes 1,800 minutes in two consecutive months after the waste pass: move to GitHub Pro (3,000 included minutes, F-5) and add the line to §6.
  • Self-hosted is rejected for v1's scale: it needs a separate always-on box that is not the production VPS — a second VPS-class cost (§6) plus a second machine to patch — to save at most the Pro fee. It is revisited only if minutes pass what Pro's allowance comfortably holds, at which point the comparison is redone with real numbers.

Failure mode, stated. If the allowance is exhausted mid-month at $0 limit, PR gates stop running and merges wait for the reset (or the Act trigger is pulled early). That is acceptable for a solo developer for days; it is not acceptable silently — which is why the weekly reading exists. The nightly E2E is the first thing thinned (every second night) if rationing is ever needed, because the PR smoke subset still gates every merge.

18. Failure handling

Delivery and operations failures — the things that go wrong around the system rather than inside it. Module-level failure behaviour stays in Docs 03–08; this section covers the plan's own seams.

Deploy fails mid-migration. Covered by Doc 09 §17.2 and TN-15: the deploy halts, migrated schemas serve the old code shapes they are compatible with by the expand/contract rule, unmigrated schemas are untouched. The operator's part is §11.1 step 4: fix forward or roll back (§11.2); the one forbidden act is hand-patching a schema, because it makes that tenant's state unreproducible by any later migration.

The VPS dies. Doc 04 §14.3 owns the procedure (new VPS, Compose up, public dump first, tenants in order; platform RTO 4 hours). The plan's part is that the procedure's inputs exist off-VPS by construction: backups in R2 (§14.3 drills them), the age identity offline (§15), the domain at an independent registrar (§5.1), the code on GitHub (Doc 09). A dead VPS destroys nothing irreplaceable; that sentence is true only because §12's gates check each input before the pilot.

A provider disappears or reprices. Each paid-or-free dependency has its exit already written: email (§8.3's SES path — a credential swap, because Doc 06 is provider-agnostic), VPS (§5.2's fallback — the stack is provider-agnostic), Sentry (§7.3 — self-hosting is the documented, deferred alternative), CI (§17 — the pipeline is host-shaped, not host-owned, per Doc 09 AS-2). The common pattern is the defence: no provider named in this document is load-bearing in the code.

The free-tier cap is hit mid-day. Email: §8.2 — sends fail softly, rows age, sweeps retry, the tier moves within the week. Sentry: events past quota are dropped by Sentry, not by the application (Doc 08 §11.4's failure mode) — the platform keeps working blind for the remainder of the month, which is exactly why §7.3 acts at 80% projection instead of at the wall. CI: §17 — merges pause, visibly.

The operator is unavailable. Short absences: the cadence slips, alerts still fire, nothing in the design needs daily human hands (§14.1). Extended absence: §15's continuity mechanism. The platform's obligations that cannot slip silently — domain and VPS billing — are the ones §5.1 and §6 put on multi-year terms and monthly auto-paid ceilings respectively; both are called out on the continuity card.

A pilot tenant wants out. Offboarding is Doc 04 §4's procedure (suspend → export → 30-day grace → schema drop), with Doc 08 §13's retention answers. The pilot adds one rule: a pilot exit interview, however brief, and its notes filed with the capacity log — at pilot scale, why a lab left is the most valuable data the platform produces.

23. Interview talking points

  • Why does the delivery plan refuse to print a domain name and an exact VPS price? Because both are purchase-day facts (availability, the June 2026 repricing, F-1/F-7), and a document that prints them trains the reader to trust stale numbers. The plan fixes everything that makes the purchase safe — TLD class, renewal class, 5-year term, machine class, measurement procedure, fallback — and records the day-of facts where they belong: the capacity log and the receipt (§5, §6).
  • Why is "no staggering" the right answer to the settlement fan-out? Because the scary version of the problem (100 tenants emailing statements at 01:00) is impossible in the design as written: generation creates drafts, and humans approve them in daylight (Doc 05). The load that does exist — renders at 01:00 — is already governed by Doc 04's per-tenant caps, and the run is measured by Doc 05's own log against a written trigger (§10). Staggering would be a solution performing diligence about a problem the architecture already dissolved.
  • Why are all the scaling triggers "any one of several measures" instead of one clean number? Because load has several shapes — many tenants, one huge tenant, slow migrations, hot reads — and a single metric fires late for the shapes it does not measure. OQ-40 itself named three measures; §7.2 takes all three plus latency, any one sufficient, all read off one weekly log. The cost of a trigger firing slightly early is a bigger VPS; the cost of firing late is tenants feeling it first.
  • Why is Sentry's trigger a quota percentage and not a tenant count, when OQ-52 asked for a tenant count? Because the honest answer to the question as asked is that the premise is wrong: with unsampled error events, quota burn is a property of bugs, not of tenants (§7.3). Giving a tenant count anyway would be inventing a fact to fit the question. The trigger the operator can actually watch — projected events vs 5,000 — is stated instead, with the paid step priced.
  • Why does the launch checklist gate on a restore drill rather than on backups "being configured"? A backup that has never been restored is a hope with a schedule. Doc 04's RTO is a promise to tenants; DL-04/DL-05 prove the promise on the real stack before a real lab depends on it, and §14.3 re-proves it monthly. Configuration is a state; a drill is evidence (DP-6).
  • Why is the continuity holder forbidden to operate anything? Because the failure being planned for is absence, not incompetence: what the platform needs from the holder is custody and a clean handover, legible to a non-technical person under stress. A holder who could operate would need training, access reviews and trust decisions the solo model cannot maintain — and would become a second, unmonitored operator, which Doc 08's threat model would then have to price (§15).
  • Why gate phases on test suites instead of dates? In a schema-per-tenant system, defects compound by tenant count: a broken phase discovered after ten tenants are provisioned is repaired ten times. A date can be met by lowering the bar invisibly; a named suite going green cannot (DP-2, Doc 09 §9.2). The estimates are published as forecasts precisely so they can be wrong without the gates moving.
  • Why set the GitHub spending limit to $0 — isn't a stopped pipeline worse than a small bill? For a solo developer the failure that matters is the silent one: minutes quietly billing, or quota quietly eaten by a looping nightly job. A $0 limit converts overage into a stopped, visible, fixable event during a weekly review that already reads the number (§17). The bill it risks is bounded either way; the attention failure is not.
